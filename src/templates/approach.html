Approach

Preservation and innovation are usually staged as a tug of war. This site treats them as two axes instead. The projects worth studying score high on both, in the upper-right corner.

1. Weigh the values

Every reuse negotiates between preservation and innovation. Owners, authorities and designers weigh use, spatial quality and heritage value differently. Their agreed weighting sets how far a design may go.

2. Choose the use

A building cannot become just anything. Each candidate use is tested on two criteria: can the building carry it, and does it make something new?

Can the building carry it? Typology matching treats a type as a spatial pattern, not a function label. Name the trait the new use cannot do without, then measure the gap against references of the same scale. What fits is repaired, what falls short is added, what conflicts is removed.

Worked example: two harbour warehouses to a cruise terminal (thesis design proposal A+A′ (opens in a new tab))

FOA's Yokohama terminal sets out the trait that defines a cruise terminal: embarking and disembarking as two one-way flows that never turn back. But Yokohama is far larger than the warehouses, so it shows the principle, not the fit.

The two Keelung Harbour warehouses were therefore compared with terminals of their own size in Lisbon and Salerno, which follow the same principle. Conversion proved possible, but the warehouses' circulation had to be reorganised to provide the two flows.

Does it make something new? Feasibility makes reuse competent, not memorable. The memorable cases pair a space with a use it was never meant for, and the mismatch produces a form or atmosphere neither had alone. Tschumi (1994) called this crossprogramming. Across the case studies here, four contrasts recur:

  1. Users: who it was built for, and who arrives now.
  2. Cultural context: the meanings the old use carried, and the new.
  3. Scale: what the space was sized for, and what it holds now.
  4. Speed: how long people stay and how fast they move, from storage to passage.

Too little contrast is forgettable; too much becomes a gimmick or costs heritage value. Good contrasts come from an honest survey of what a city lacks, a survey architects are rarely invited to make.

3. Read the building

Once a use is chosen, form analysis reads the building against what that use needs: what must survive, what cannot change, and where design is actually needed.

The categories follow Ching's Architecture: Form, Space, and Order (2007) and Simitch and Warke's The Language of Architecture (2014).

Massing
How is the volume composed, and what can be added or taken away?
Structure
What spans, grids and loads does the building allow?
Construction and material
What is it made of, and in what condition?
Rhythm
What repetition orders the plan and the facade?
Circulation
How do people enter, move through and leave?
Urban context
How does it meet the street, the block, the water?
Hierarchy
Which spaces lead, and which serve?
Light and view
Where does light enter, and what can be seen?
Openings
Where can the envelope be opened, and where not?

Not every case needs all nine; the new use decides which matter. A+A′ analysed six: massing, structure, rhythm, section, circulation and urban context.

4. Calibrate the intervention

Every case is placed on one scale, from non-intervention to relocation.

Adaptive reuse works in the marked span, where space or volume changes but the building stays in place. The final check: does the design's position on the scale match the weighting agreed in step 1?

5. How each case is written

Every case has the same five parts, so any two can be compared.

  1. Introduction presents the building.
  2. Reuse Goal sets out the new use and why it fits.
  3. Challenge names what the reading revealed.
  4. Adaptive Strategy states the principle of intervention.
  5. Outcome shows the space and atmosphere it produced, and judges whether it works.

What this lens leaves out

This is an architect's lens at building scale. Finance, regulation and politics also decide outcomes; cases note them where they matter.
