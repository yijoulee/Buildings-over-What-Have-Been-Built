  introduction:
    body: Kaispeicher A was built in 1963–1966 to designs by the post-war modernist Werner Kallmorgen, on the Kaiserhöft, the western tip of the Großer Grasbrook quay. It is a closed red-brick block about 30 m high on a trapezoidal plan, whose regular grid of small 50 × 75 cm openings reads more as structural pattern than as windows. It was the only warehouse in the port on deep-water berths, and portal cranes lifted overseas goods, mainly cocoa, straight from the ships. Containerisation reduced demand from the early 1970s, and the warehouse fell out of use around 1990. Its brick facade is a listed monument.

  reuse_goal:
    body: The site marks the western end of HafenCity, whose 2000 masterplan extends Hamburg's inner city by about 40 % onto former port land and leads it back to the Elbe. In 2001 the architect Alexander Gérard and the art historian Jana Marko proposed putting a concert hall on top of the empty warehouse instead, and in 2003 the City of Hamburg commissioned Herzog & de Meuron. The aim was a landmark for the new district and a centre of social, cultural and everyday life, open to the public and not only to concertgoers. Alongside the concert halls, the brief combined a five-star hotel, private apartments, restaurants, conference facilities and parking. Only the warehouse's brick facade was to stay, as the visual base of the new building, while a new structure built inside it would carry the loads.

  challenges:
    body: |-
      - **Foundation load**: a building of about 200,000 t had to stand on a 1960s warehouse site. The 1,111 original piles were supplemented with more than 600 new reinforced-concrete piles, micropiles were added in problem areas, and the floor slab was strengthened with extra supports. 
      - **Acoustic block**: a concert hall had to share one building with a hotel and homes, on a working harbour. Low-frequency ship horns must not reach the halls as structure-borne noise, and loud music must not reach the hotel and flats. 
      - **Parametric design and fabrication**: some 10,000 individually shaped acoustic panels and 1,100 glass facade elements, 595 of them curved, demanded digital design and fabrication at a scale few buildings had attempted. 
      - **Cost and schedule overrun**: the city's initial share of about €77 million grew to an official total of about €866 million. Work stopped from October 2011 to May 2012, and a parliamentary inquiry blamed an early tender based on incomplete planning.

  adaptive_strategy:
    body: |-
      The project worked through extrusion and stacking: in 2007 the warehouse was gutted down to its outer walls, and a new load-bearing structure was built inside them on strengthened foundations. The listed brick facade now acts as a plinth only visually. Above it, a new glass volume was extruded from the warehouse's exact footprint, with a public plaza as the joint between old and new. 

      - **A landmark at the tip**: the new volume rises from about 80 m at the east end to about 110 m at the western point of the peninsula. For anyone arriving along the Elbe, it marks the arrival in the city. Its wave-shaped roof of eight concave spherical sections, covered in about 6,000 reflective sequins, is treated as a fifth facade. A star-shaped steel frame carries the roof, about 8,000 t, across the Grand Hall without internal columns. 
      - **Glass against brick**: the single-skin facade of curved, individually formed glass panels reflects the water and picks up the waves of the river, while the closed brick base stays solid. The contrast lets the original and the extension each keep their own character rather than merging into one. Grey and chrome dots printed on each pane, calculated pane by pane, control solar gain and privacy. The openings follow the uses behind them: gill-like hatches for the hotel and foyers, and tuning-fork-shaped recesses for the apartment loggias at the western tip. 
      - **The "tube", from city to harbour**: the main entrance sits at the east end, on the city side. From there an escalator about 82 m long runs through the warehouse, rising 21 m with a gradient that flattens from 23° to about 11°. Its concave curve hides the end from the start, and the tunnel is lined with reflective glass sequins. It ends at a panoramic window onto the harbour. The ride restages, at the scale of one building, HafenCity's aim of leading the city to the water. 
      - **Public plaza**: at 37 m, on the 8th floor, a public plaza roughly the size of the square in front of Hamburg's town hall runs between the warehouse roof and the new volume, open with or without a ticket. A walkway wraps around the outside, giving views on all sides. It is paved with about 188,000 red bricks that echo the warehouse and connects the hotel lobby, restaurants, ticket office and concert foyers. Vaulted ceilings divide it into zones. 
      - **Room within a room**: both concert halls are independent structures floating on steel springs, 362 under the Grand Hall and 56 under the Small Hall, tuned to 4.5 Hz and 3.5 Hz. In the Grand Hall, 2,100 seats rise in vineyard-style tiers around the stage, so no seat is more than 30 m from the conductor.

  solutions:
    body: 'The Elbphilharmonie was handed over on 31 October 2016 and inaugurated with concerts in January 2017. Behind the brick facade lie the parking garage, the hotel spa, conference rooms and backstage areas. The glass volume above holds the two concert halls, the hotel’s 244 rooms on the east side and 45 apartments at the western tip. Herzog & de Meuron turned an anonymous harbour warehouse into the plinth of a landmark that presents HafenCity to the river, while the free public plaza made the building part of everyday city life. The project is also a cautionary example: a reuse can involve a more complex construction process than a new build, and when construction starts without comprehensive planning, an ambitious reuse can escalate in cost.'
