  introduction:
    body: Built between 1899 and 1901 to a design by the justice architect W.C. Metzelaar, the Haarlem Koepel is one of three Dutch dome prisons based on Bentham's panopticon. It is a circular building in which four stacked rings of cells (240 in total) line the outer wall along open galleries, all facing a single central hall where a guard post stood beneath a large dome roof. The dome stood on an open plain inside a prison wall, together with an administration building and the House of Detention "De Vest", while the directors' and guards' houses lay outside the wall. From the 1960s, when the regime shifted towards re-education, the site filled up with workrooms, sports halls, warehouses, kitchens, isolation cells and a caged sports field, and the hall itself received a guard unit, a lift tower and a wash tower. The dome is a national monument (rijksmonument). It closed as a prison in 2016, after inmate numbers fell and budgets were cut, and briefly served as a shelter for Syrian refugees.

  reuse_goal:
    body: 'When the State sold the complex, the municipality passed the dome on to the local citizens'' initiative Stichting Panopticon, which set out to hand it back to the city under the motto "Open De Koepel". The municipality wanted university-level education in Haarlem, a long-standing wish, and saw the project as the anchor of its "Sprong over het Spaarne" plan to extend the old centre across the river into Haarlem-Oost. The brief therefore combined a university college, a cinema and workspace for small businesses with a fully public ground floor. The heritage stance was "function follows form": the building''s shape decided which uses could move in, interventions were to stay restrained and functional, and the traces of its prison history were to remain readable.'

  challenges:
    body: |-
      - **Built to stay closed:** ​a building designed to keep inmates in and the public out had to become an inviting, publicly accessible part of the city. 
      - **Not enough usable space:** ​the brief needed large halls for cinema and lectures that the ring of small cells could not provide, and the historic foundations also needed repair. 
      - **A giant sound box:** ​the hard, empty hall under the dome had a reverberation time of about 7 seconds, and the heritage rules allowed no new windows in the roof. 
      - **Cells as workplaces:** ​the 240 cells were small and dark behind barred cast-iron windows, yet they had to become comfortable offices and study rooms without changing the protected exterior.

  adaptive_strategy:
    body: |-
      The project worked through subtraction and infill: later additions were stripped away to bring back the original panoptic space, and a new building was then set freely into the hall and dug into the ground beneath it. 

      \- \*\*Value map and subtraction:\*\* a heritage value map decided what to keep. All monumental buildings were restored, while the post-1960s annexes were demolished, along with the guard unit, lift tower and wash tower inside the dome. A colour study recovered the original colours of the cell doors, railings and roof trusses, and a demolished bay window was rebuilt as the focal point of the new Koepelplein. Where an annex had been torn off the facade, the exposed opening became the new main entrance, filled with tall glazing. 

      - **Basement dug top-down:** diaphragm walls were sunk along the inner wall and new piles driven, then the ground-floor slab was poured directly on the sand, leaving a round opening 16 m across where the stair would later go. The basement was excavated through this opening to about 5 m deep while the steel structure rose above it, so the building grew up and down at the same time, which shortened construction by more than a year. Once new load-bearing inner walls had taken over part of the loads, some of the temporary piles could be removed again. The resulting concrete basin doubles as the foundation repair, its upward pressure countering subsidence, and holds six radially arranged halls (600 seats in total) used for lectures in the morning and as the Filmkoepel cinema for the rest of the day. 
      - **Freestanding steel infill and stacked programme:** a circular, three-layer steel structure with large glass facades stands a few metres in from the galleries, linked to them by walkways. It holds work and meeting rooms without touching the cell ring, so the full dome remains visible from everywhere. The programme is layered by its need for daylight: the basement takes the windowless cinema halls and plant rooms, the ground floor is a public "agora" ring of café, bookshop and entrances around the central opening, and the three upper floors of cells and steel structure hold the university college and workspaces.
      - **Cells opened with care:** each cell gained sound-insulating walls and a new deep-set vertical window with insulating glass below the barred window, while the bars stay in place outside. Access still runs along the original galleries and spiral stairs, with two new stairwells added. The original balustrades were kept, made safe with fine stainless-steel cable netting. 
      - **Invisible acoustic ceiling:** the underside of the zinc roof was first insulated with about 8 cm of sprayed PUR. Below it, about 2,700 m² of prefabricated, insulated acoustic boxes (another 8 cm) were fixed between the existing rafters and covered with fabric printed with photographs of the original ceiling, so the dome looks unchanged from below. Reverberation dropped from about 7 to 1.4 seconds. 
      - **Climate through the monument:** an aquifer thermal energy storage system (WKO) supplies heating and cooling, and the cell ring acts as a thermal buffer around the hall. Two heat-recovery ventilation systems use the building's own fabric. For the interior rooms, large units in the basement plant rooms draw fresh air in through the existing chimney flues and exhaust it through the original ventilation wells (koekoeken), with two large vertical ducts serving three floors; in the cinema halls air is blown in through shafts and returns through the central opening. Small units under the roof serve the cells and draw return air from the hall through the original cast-iron ventilation grilles. Old service-riser shafts were reused for fresh-air supply, and power is plugged in from busbars at the top of the dome down to each cell.

  solutions:
    body: Since March 2022, the Koepel has housed Haarlem's university college, the Filmkoepel cinema, co-working space for start-ups and small businesses, a game museum and an exhibition space. A panopticon built for surveillance has become a public living room for the city. The project shows that a centralized prison type can stay almost intact when the new programme goes into the empty centre and beneath it, rather than into the cells alone. The architects describe the building as nearly energy-neutral, thanks to WKO, heat recovery and the cell ring acting as a thermal buffer.
