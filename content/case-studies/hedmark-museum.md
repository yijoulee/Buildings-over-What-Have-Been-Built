sections:
  introduction:
    body: The Hedmark Museum (1967–1979), designed by Sverre Fehn, is located on a lakeside promontory west of Hamar, Norway. The museum is built within the remains of a medieval bishop’s palace and a later barn that incorporated parts of the medieval ruins into its walls. Today the complex holds archaeological finds alongside Hedmark’s regional folk collections, shown in display fittings Fehn designed together with the building.
  reuse_goal:
    body: The project aims to transform the historic ruins into a museum while preserving their physical integrity and historical character. Old and new are kept visibly distinct, and neither is disguised as the other. Radford describes the relationship as “a conversation, a responsive cohesion” (Radford et al., 2014). The goal is not to complete the ruin but to make it usable as it is. The new structures carry the museum’s programme, so the old walls never have to be rebuilt to serve it.
  challenges:
    body: |-
      The primary challenge was to accommodate a functional museum within an archaeological site without damaging or obscuring its historic remains. The existing stone walls were fragmented and irregular, while the ground needed to remain largely undisturbed to allow archaeological excavation to continue.

      The project also had to reconcile different spatial requirements, including exhibition galleries, an auditorium, circulation routes, and outdoor viewing areas, within the constraints of the existing ruins.

  adaptive_strategy:
    body: |-
      Radford et al. (2014) read the building through four principal elements: the ruin, the concrete platforms, the timber roof and the steel display mounts. Each one answers one of the challenges above.

      - **Minimal ground intervention:** Visitors walk on concrete decks raised on columns above the excavated floor, so archaeologists can keep digging beneath the public route. 
      - **Circulation and spatial organisation:** Circulation is a continuous promenade rather than a series of rooms. Its most visible gesture is the concrete ramp that swings out over the courtyard dig, turning the excavation itself into something seen from above. The auditorium occupies the west wing, while raised platforms and exhibition areas extend through the east wing.
      - **Material expression and lighting:** Board-marked concrete is set against rubble stone. The timber-truss roof is covered in clay tiles with glass tiles set among them, so daylight enters in scattered patches and the interior stays dim. Fehn leaves the edge unrepaired and fixes a frameless glass pane over the gap, so the damage stays visible.
      - **Integrated exhibition design:** The displays use a small kit of black steel and glass, but every mount is shaped around a single object and is often anchored straight into the old masonry. Three enclosed concrete cells, each lifted on one column, give the smallest finds a neutral setting away from the ruins.

  solutions:
    body: |-
      Hedmark Museum demonstrates how contemporary architecture can coexist with archaeological remains without reconstructing or overwhelming the historic fabric. Rather than treating the ruins as a backdrop for a new museum, Fehn allows them to remain an essential part of the architecture. The project illustrates how minimal intervention can transform an archaeological site into an inhabitable cultural space while keeping every historical layer in view.

      The contrast between the irregular stone walls and the precise geometry of the new concrete, timber, and steel elements makes the distinction between old and new clearly legible. At the same time, the integration of structural design, natural lighting, and exhibition fittings creates a coherent architectural and curatorial experience.

      **Reference**
      Radford, A., Srivastava, A. & Morkoç, S. (2014). _The Elements of Modern Architecture: Understanding Contemporary Buildings_. London: Thames & Hudson.
