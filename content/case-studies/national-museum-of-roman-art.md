  introduction:
    body: |-
      The National Museum of Roman Art in Mérida, Spain, was commissioned in 1979 as part of the celebrations marking the 2,000th anniversary of the founding of the Roman city of Emerita Augusta. 

      Located within one of the best-preserved Roman archaeological areas in Western Europe, the project occupies a site containing extensive remains, including aqueducts, Roman houses, courtyards, cisterns, drainage systems, and an early Christian church. It also stands near the city's Roman theatre and amphitheatre. Architect Rafael Moneo adopted the recurring arches found throughout the archaeological site as the museum's defining architectural motif.

  reuse_goal:
    body: The project aims to create a museum that both preserves and exhibits the archaeological remains of ancient Mérida. The museum was conceived as a link between different historical periods, representing not only the city's Roman heritage but also its continuing urban history. Its architecture draws upon Roman construction traditions while employing contemporary design principles to accommodate modern museum functions.
  challenges:
    body: |-
      The main challenge was to construct a museum on a site densely occupied by archaeological remains while preserving their physical and historical significance. The discovery of extensive ruins during excavation required the original project, initially conceived as a retaining wall, to be reconsidered as a much larger architectural intervention.

      Another challenge concerned the relationship between the ancient Roman urban grid and the orientation of contemporary Mérida. The design had to negotiate these competing spatial references while accommodating the museum programme and minimising direct structural interference with the archaeological remains.

  adaptive_strategy:
    body: |-
      Moneo developed an architectural language that reinterprets Roman construction techniques through contemporary forms and spatial arrangements.

      - Minimising physical impact: Using the grid of the ancient Roman city as a reference, Moneo slightly rotated the building's axis to minimise the number of new structural columns directly in contact with the archaeological remains.
      - Structural system and material expression: Massive load-bearing walls are constructed from elongated Roman-style bricks with concrete infill, creating a sense of solidity associated with ancient Roman buildings. The carefully controlled brickwork and thin mortar joints reduce visual distraction, allowing the archaeological artefacts to remain prominent.
      - Architectural reference and archaeological integration: The museum combines expansive above-ground exhibition galleries with a subterranean exhibition space, or crypt, where visitors can experience the excavated remains in their original context, including an ancient Roman road. A subterranean passage further connects the museum with nearby Roman monuments. The upper galleries feature a central nave-like space articulated by soaring brick arches. Its repeated semicircular arches establish a visual connection with the archaeological surroundings while giving the museum a distinct contemporary identity.
