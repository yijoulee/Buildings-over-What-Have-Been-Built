  introduction:
    body: "The site in the south-east corner of Leeuwarden's old centre began in 1499 as a fortified blokhuis, a near-square earthwork enclosure of roughly 200 × 200 m inside a moat, and was used to hold prisoners from 1560 onward. Between 1870 and the mid-1870s the government architect J.F. Metzelaar replaced it with a new prison that largely kept the old footprint: brick buildings arranged as a double square around walled courtyards, still bounded by the city moats. His son W.C. Metzelaar extended it with the Huis van Bewaring (remand house) on the east side and a further cell wing in 1889–1894. These wings are three storeys high, each with a long central void lined by cast-iron galleries of cells. The complex became a national monument (rijksmonument) in 2000 and closed as a prison in 2007–2008, after which it served as a temporary cultural incubator."

  reuse_goal:
    body: Heritage developer BOEi bought the complex in 2014 to turn it into a public cultural meeting place for the eastern inner city, ready for Leeuwarden-Fryslân's year as European Capital of Culture in 2018. The ambition was to bring the central city library, creative businesses, a hostel and hospitality inside the walls while keeping the prison's enclosed, utilitarian character readable.

  challenges:
    body: |-
      - **A building designed for isolation had to become public**: high walls, a moat and closed facades had cut the complex off from the city for five centuries. 
      - **The cell grain fits only part of the new programme**: cells can take hostel rooms and workshops with small changes, but a public library needs open floors, daylight and a front door to the city.
      - **The monument carries layers of alteration**: later interventions, including recent ones and a collapsed section of prison wall, were treated as part of the story rather than errors to undo.
  adaptive_strategy:
    body: |-
      TWA worked by selective incision and legible insertion, guided by one principle: in a complex with repeated building types, one specimen can be conserved so that its duplicates can be transformed. Most of the fabric was kept, openings were cut only where the public programme needed them, and most of addition was marked in minimal black steel and glass. 

      - **One wing restored, the twin transformed**: the Huis van Bewaring and the Strafgevangenis share the same cell-hall type, so the first was restored close to its original state while the second absorbed the major intervention. Programme was allocated by both form fit and urban position: cell-sized uses went into the restored wing, and the library, which needed open floors and a public face, took the twin at the city-facing corner beside the gatehouse. 
      - **Library in the Strafgevangenis wing (1894)**: the middle of the wing was taken out and replaced by a glass-fronted volume holding the new entrance; a low superstructure on the ridge adds a roof terrace, a mostly glazed extension fills the strip between the wing and the prison wall without rising above it, and the wall itself received only a few selective openings, keeping its closed character and the collapsed west section. 
      - **Huis van Bewaring (1889–1891) as craft street and hostel**: the ground-floor corridor became a street for creative businesses and the upper cells became hostel rooms; a new intermediate cell gives two neighbouring cells a shared bathroom, and in the former women's wing on the top floor, cells were joined through wider openings to hold the dining hall, lounge and workspaces. 
      - **Materials exposed, colours researched**: walls and floors were cleaned back to brick and the original tiles, painted surfaces returned to the last historic yellow-and-blue scheme, and some cells were left untouched as museum cells. 
      - **The 1948 smithy as entrance landmark**: blind arched niches in the side facade were opened with slender steel frames set flush to the brick, the front pier rhythm was kept, and the raised guard post became a meeting room.
