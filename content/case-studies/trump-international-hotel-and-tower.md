  introduction:
    body: |-
      During the late 1980s and early 1990s, many American cities experienced an outflow of businesses and residents to suburban areas. As a result, an increasing number of office buildings became functionally obsolete, further weakening the economic vitality of central business districts (CBDs). Economic recession and the downturn in the property market intensified the decline of urban centres.

      Located at Columbus Circle, Manhattan, the 52-storey Trump International Hotel and Tower was originally built in 1968 as an office building. The redevelopment retained its existing steel structural frame and converted the 538,700 sq ft (approximately 50,047 m²) tower into a mixed-use development comprising 158 residential apartments and 168 hotel units.
  reuse_goal:
    body: |-
      With declining federal funding and limited subsidies available from local governments, municipalities increasingly recognised the private sector's role in rehabilitating and reusing existing buildings as a key strategy for revitalising central business districts. Local governments therefore sought to encourage the conversion of obsolete commercial buildings into residential and mixed-use developments, typically combining apartments on upper floors with retail and office spaces at ground level.

      The project aimed to transform an obsolete office tower into a luxury residential and hospitality development, making use of its prominent location overlooking Central Park and the potential of its existing structure.
  challenges:
    body: |-
      The primary design challenge in converting office buildings into residential spaces was to maximise the usable interior floor area while accommodating the different spatial requirements of residential functions. In particular, the central core (without natural lighting) of existing office buildings had to be used efficiently to improve the proportion of usable space.

      - Interior configuration: The relatively small floorplates of approximately 1,200 sq m required efficient spatial planning to maximise usable residential areas.
      - Structural performance: The original asymmetrical tower was prone to excessive wind-induced movement, requiring structural improvements to meet residential requirements.
      - Regulatory constraints: The existing building exceeded the permitted residential floor-area ratio and had nonconforming height and setback conditions, limiting the scope of redevelopment.
      - Existing building conditions: The removal of asbestos-containing fireproofing and outdated building systems added complexity to the renovation.
  adaptive_strategy:
    body: |-
      - Structural retention: The building was stripped back to its original steel frame, while the exterior walls, windows, and major building systems were replaced. Structural reinforcement was introduced to reduce wind-induced movement.
      - Mixed-use conversion: The building was divided into residential apartments and condominium hotel units to comply with zoning restrictions on residential floor area. Separate entrances and building services accommodated the two functions.
      - Interior reconfiguration: The relatively compact floorplates and existing structural column arrangement were used to create efficient residential layouts. High floor-to-floor heights allowed finished ceilings of approximately 3-4.5m, while the deep spaces between the central core and exterior windows accommodated walk-in closets, dressing rooms, kitchens, and larger entrance areas. Living rooms were positioned at the building's corners to maximise views, with approximately 80% of the residential units overlooking Central Park.
      - Infrastructure improvements: Local governments introduced public infrastructure update to support renewed residential and commercial activities.

  solutions:
    body: |-
      The project transformed an obsolete office tower into a luxury mixed-use development while retaining its original steel structural frame. Through structural rehabilitation, façade replacement, and interior reorganisation, it demonstrates how an existing high-rise office building can accommodate new residential and hospitality functions while capitalising on its original structural and locational advantages.

      More broadly, certain characteristics of former office buildings make them particularly suitable for residential conversion. Relatively small floorplates, large operable windows, high ceilings, and unobstructed views provide favourable conditions for creating residential layouts.

      The conversion of former office buildings into apartments and lofts has gradually emerged as a form of urban housing that integrates living and working spaces. Such developments can also attract residents back from suburban areas, reducing commuting time and energy consumption.

      **Reference** Gause, Jo Allen. (1996). New uses for obsolete buildings. Washington, D.C.: Urban Land Institute.
