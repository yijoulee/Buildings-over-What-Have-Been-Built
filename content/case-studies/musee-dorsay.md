  introduction:
    body: |-
      The Musée d’Orsay is located along the Seine River, facing the Louvre across the water. It was originally built in 1900 as the Gare d'Orsay railway station, a major transportation hub in Paris that included an integrated railway hotel. Like the almost contemporaneous Eiffel Tower, the station symbolized France’s industrial and engineering prowess.

      Its vast platform hall was formed using long-span cast-iron trusses (32m high, 40m wide, 140m long), creating a tall and transparent volume. The metal framework allowed a higher interior space, generous window openings, and a glass roof that flooded the station with sunlight, producing a bright and luxurious spatial atmosphere.

  reuse_goal:
    body: |-
      By the 1970s, the railway station had been abandoned. During the redevelopment competition, the architects aimed to preserve the historic structure while redefining the building’s relationship to the Seine’s urban fabric.

      Another core goal was to transform the highly functional station into an art museum, suitable for exhibition, circulation, and visitor flow.

  challenges:
    body: |-
      In the original station layout, seven arches of various sizes faced the Seine and served as entrances/exits designed for efficient passenger flow. These doorways cut laterally across the platforms, enabling rapid circulation—suitable for a train station but unsuitable for a museum.

      The challenge was:

      * How to convert a multi-entry, flow-oriented transit building into a single-entry museum with controlled circulation.
      * While minimizing interventions and preserving the architectural character.

  adaptive_strategy:
    body: |-
      A.C.T. Architecture repositioned the entrance to the western plaza, orienting the main access along the longitudinal axis to meet museum functional requirements through a single controlled entry point.

      Spatial strategies included:

      * Maintaining the overall order of the original station plan
      * Transforming the train platforms into a central ground-floor gallery
      * Using the mid-level terraces to overlook the central hall, which connect to additional exhibition rooms
      * Organizing galleries at multiple vertical levels, running parallel to each other
      * Using arches of different spans to define spatial directionality and gradations of scale (large, medium, small), thereby clarifying circulation and the relationship between main and secondary exhibition spaces.

  solutions:
    body: |-
      The reuse project focused on preserving the open, flowing spatial atmosphere created by the long-span cast-iron trusses and extensive glass roof.

      By making minimal yet strategic modifications, especially the reorientation of the main entrance, the architects successfully adapted the station’s spatial logic into a museum typology without compromising the identity of the original building.
