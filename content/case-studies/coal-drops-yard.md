  introduction:
    body: "The coal drops at King's Cross were built by the Great Northern Railway to transfer coal from the north of England onto London's carts and barges. The Eastern Coal Drops (1851) are a long brick shed of 48 bays: trains ran in on four tracks at the top level, wagon floors opened, and the coal fell through chutes to a hopper floor on cast-iron columns and beams, from where it was sorted down to road vehicles at ground level. The Western Coal Drops (1860) followed a simpler, more advanced design with a 15 m roof span, their rails carried on an open cast-iron viaduct above a basin for loading canal boats, which was filled in in 1897–99. The two sheds stand almost parallel, slightly angled to each other, with a long yard between them, and to the south the Wharf Road Viaduct closes the site along the Regent's Canal. Later used as warehouses, light industry and nightclubs, the listed sheds lost much of their timber structure in a fire in 1985 and largely fell out of use from the late 1990s."

  reuse_goal:
    body: Argent, the developer of the wider King's Cross Central regeneration, appointed Heatherwick Studio in 2014 to restore the two sheds and turn them into a retail destination and a new public space for the district. The site had always been closed off, and the brief was to open it to the public for the first time, with the yard between the sheds as its public heart. The historic character of the brick, cast iron and cobbles was to be kept, while the shops would serve established brands, emerging labels and graduates from the neighbouring Central Saint Martins art school.
  challenges:
    body: |-
      - **Two separate buildings**: the sheds stood apart on a long, closed linear plot, and a conventional rooftop box would have clashed with their gabled roofs. 
      - **Damaged and undocumented fabric**: the fire had destroyed most of the original timber structure, only a few cast-iron beams and columns survived in unknown condition, and no original structural drawings existed, so each construction phase had to be reconstructed from site evidence. 
      - **Limited load capacity**: the surviving frame could take hardly any additional load, yet the brief called for a new upper storey and a large roof. 
      - **Listed heritage**: every intervention had to be agreed with Historic England and the local authority while keeping as much of the existing structure as possible.

  adaptive_strategy:
    body: |-
      The project worked through extension and linking: the existing sheds were restored with a light touch, and their inner gabled roofs were stretched towards each other until they meet, joining the two buildings under one new roof. 

      - **Kissing roofs:** ​the inner gables of the two two-storey sheds rise and twist towards each other and touch above the yard, forming a roof about 35 m wide with a 32 m clear span. Beneath the meeting point they create a floating glazed upper storey and a sheltered, double-height covered square for events and performances. The roof turns the yard, once a linear service space between two rows of bays, into a defined centre that the shops gather around. 
      - **Rooftop garden on the viaduct:** ​the roof of the Wharf Road Viaduct at the southern end of the site was turned into a planted rooftop garden. At first-floor level it connects to the balconies along the inner faces of both sheds, so a continuous upper level wraps the square on three sides and encloses it more strongly than the buildings alone. 
      - **Independent structure:** ​a new steel and concrete frame is threaded through internal bays of the old sheds on new mini-piles, with 54 steel columns embedded in the building, so the new roof and upper level put no load on the historic walls. The upper floor hangs from the roof on steel hangers, and the steelwork was optimised in a shared BIM model. 
      - **Matching and patinated materials:** ​the new roofs are covered in slate from the same seam and Welsh quarry as the originals. Soot-stained brick, aged ironwork, timber boards, the cobbled yard and even later signage and graffiti were kept.

  solutions:
    body: Coal Drops Yard opened on 26 October 2018 and turned a closed industrial site into a public yard at the centre of King's Cross. Entrances at both ends and along Stable Street make the yard permeable, joining the viaducts and the yard into one public space lined with close to retail 60 units. Above, the new upper level is enclosed by an 8 m tall folded glass facade that spans between slabs and trusses. The project shows how a new roof can join two heritage buildings into one place without putting any of its load on the historic fabric.
