  introduction:
    body: Eduard Louis de Coninck designed this freestanding neoclassical Palace of Justice in 1838, and it was built by 1841 on the Blijmarkt, between Zwolle's medieval centre and the 19th-century park along the canals. The plan is a rectangle, symmetrical on both axes, with a columned portico on each long side. A narrow axial hall runs between the two porticoes and rises through two storeys. On either side of it sits a large square hall, ringed by a corridor and a band of smaller perimeter rooms. After serving as a courthouse and later as offices of the national planning agency, it was turned into an art museum through an interior renovation by Gunnar Daan in 2004–2005.

  reuse_goal:
    body: Under director Ralph Keuning, a run of successful temporary exhibitions left the museum short of gallery space. In 2010 he asked Hubert-Jan Henket to advise on an extension adding about 1,000 m² of exhibition space, with the aim of showing the historic and modern collections together in a single building. The client's original brief called for a transparent annex beside the building.
  challenges:
    body: |-
      - **A side wing would have undermined the building's identity**, which depends on its double symmetry and its freestanding position. 
      - **Building underground was not an option**, because of the historic foundations and the awkward routing it would have created.
      - **A new volume of 1,000 m² had to be added to a listed monument** while keeping its classical character intact.
  adaptive_strategy:
    body: |-
      Henket turned down the side annex and went upward instead, placing an autonomous, contrasting volume on the roof so that the old and new buildings each keep their own value. 

      - **Elliptical "Wolk" (cloud)**: a rugby-ball shape about 33 × 22 m, aligned with the palace's long axis and centred on it. It nearly fills the depth of the block, leaving the corners and porticoes free, and is set back about 2 m from the eaves so it appears to float. 
      - **Independent structure**: a steel frame on eight steel columns passes through the old building down to its own bored-pile foundations, so the monument carries no extra load. 
      - **Atrium as hinge**: the narrow axial hall, the only void in the original plan, is extended upward as an atrium. A glass lift at its centre rises through every level into the middle of the ellipse. A glass corridor circles the junction of old and new. 
      - **Contrasting spatial language**: rectangular classical rooms and straight stairs tucked into the side bays below. Above, the upper gallery is a single open elliptical floor around the lift core, with curved stairs following the ellipse's edge. 
      - **Controlled daylight**: a single large north-facing window lights both new floors without direct sun and frames a view over the old city.

  solutions:
    body: 'The palace’s two centralized halls gain a third, vertical centre: the axial atrium continues upward into the open elliptical gallery of the Wolk. The heritage agency supported the concept from the first sketches under the principle of “preservation through development”, so the permit was granted unusually quickly. The Wolk is clad in about 55,000 three-dimensional glazed tiles, laid in a random pattern so that the volume blends with the sky. Its bold appearance has made it a landmark of the city.'
