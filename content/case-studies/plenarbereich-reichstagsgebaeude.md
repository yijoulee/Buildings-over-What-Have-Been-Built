  introduction:
    body: Originally built at the end of the 19th century to serve as the parliament building of the German Empire, the Reichstag suffered a devastating fire in 1933 and remained abandoned throughout the Cold War, when East and West Germany held their parliamentary sessions elsewhere. Only after reunification in 1990 was the building restored, with the architectural competition ultimately awarded to Norman Foster.

  reuse_goal:
    body: The project aimed to restore the Reichstag as the seat of the German parliament while acknowledging its complex architectural and political history.  A central issue in the restoration concerned the fate of the Reichstag’s historic dome, which had been destroyed by war. The winning design originally did not include reconstructing the dome. However, after the competition, some members of parliament demanded the reconstruction of the original dome.
  challenges:
    body: |-
      - **Rebuilding a lost architectural element contradicted authenticity principles**, especially since the original dome had been destroyed by war and fire.
      - **Should restoration imitate the past, or re-interpret it?**
  adaptive_strategy:
    body: |-
      Foster responded by proposing a new transparent dome rather than a historical replica. 

      - The dome features a spiraling ramp wrapped around an inner glass shell, defining the dome’s curvature and leading to an observation deck overlooking Berlin.
      - The transparent dome incorporates a glass oculus and vertical visual connection to the legislative chamber below. This gesture positions the public physically above the parliamentary chamber.
      - The design referenced the building’s architectural history symbolically and introduced a completely contemporary form, avoiding false reconstruction.

  solutions:
    body: |-
      Foster’s dome demonstrates how architecture can critically reinterpret Negative Heritage—buildings associated with authoritarian power.  This symbolic and literal transparency:

      - Allows citizens to look down into the parliament, visually supervising democracy
      - Public visitors stand above elected officials, reversing the hierarchies of the former empire
      - Transforms the dome into the building’s most public and accessible circulation space
