  introduction:
    body: 'Pakhuis Santos was built in 1901–1902 on the Katendrecht peninsula, facing the Rijnhaven, to a design by J.P. Stok and J.J. Kanters. It served as a store for coffee shipped in from Brazil: six storeys of deep, dim floors on a slender metal column frame, wrapped in ornamented Beaux-Arts façades to the north and south. After decades of storing other goods it stood empty, but its interior survived almost untouched, and it was listed as a national monument in 2000. A first conversion began in 2016, when RHWZ and WDJArchitecten designed a two-storey rooftop extension and a retail fit-out for the Hamburg design-store operator Stilwerk.'

  challenges:
    body: |-
      - **The building was made to keep light out**, with deep floor plates and small loading openings, while a public museum needs daylight and orientation. 
      - **An archive of 6.5 million photographic items demands tight climate control**, inside a listed shell with almost no insulation. 
      - **Two extra storeys load an existing column frame**, adding the weight of rooftop apartments to the original structure. 
      - **The client changed mid-construction**, so a museum programme had to be fitted into a shell and extension conceived for retail.
  adaptive_strategy:
    body: |-
      The architects kept the historic warehouse and the new work clearly apart: a void cut through the centre with the old fabric left legible throughout. Sealed boxes on the side for the collection, and a lightweight crown on top.

      - The ground floor is stripped back to its raw surfaces, and the partitions that split it into separate storage rooms are cleared, leaving one open hall across the column grid. Library, info desk, shop and café now sit freely among the columns around a stepped stage at the centre, where the atrium stair begins. 
      - A central atrium runs from the ground floor to the roof, bringing daylight deep into the plan; its stairs slightly shift position on each level, and a zigzag stair climbs into the crown. 
      - Climate is graded by floor: Box-in-box depots on the second and third floors meet archival climate requirements, and windows open them onto the visitor route so cataloguing studios and stored collection are on view. The fourth floor holds conditioned exhibition halls, while the fifth stays unconditioned to keep its raw character,
      - The two-level rooftop extension is wrapped in champagne-anodised perforated aluminium, with a parametric pattern of triangles that screens the glazing behind it and lets warm air escape from the cavity, so the crown seems to float above the monument.

  solutions:
    body: Reopened on 7 February 2026, the museum keeps the warehouse working as a store, but the storage is now what visitors come to see. The glazed crown roof and the atrium cut through the floors bring daylight into a building whose small openings once kept it dark. From the atrium, several floors are visible at once, their riveted columns and timber joists left bare. Photo walls and open depots sit within the original structure, so the building and its collection are read together.
